Method

Every connection is built from two recorded flights

The BTS on-time table lists flights, not passengers. Every connection here is constructed. At each hub, an arriving flight is paired with every departure on the same marketing airline (the name on the ticket, so a SkyWest-operated United Express leg counts as United) that leaves 30 to 180 minutes later on the schedule and goes somewhere other than where the arrival came from. Pairs flown by the same aircraft are dropped, since that plane cannot leave without its own passengers; that removed {{sameTail}}% of {{candidatesM}} million candidates, up to {{sameTailMax}}% at {{sameTailMaxHub}}. To keep every arrival equally represented, one departure is drawn at random for each arrival in each 15-minute layover band, leaving {{pairsM}} million pairs on {{hubAirlines}} hub and airline combinations. Results by layover use 5-minute bins labeled by their lower edge, so "45 minutes" means a scheduled layover of 45 to 49 minutes.

A pair is missed if the inbound was cancelled or diverted, if the outbound was cancelled, or if the inbound reached the gate too late: actual arrival plus a walk of W minutes later than actual departure minus D minutes, where D is when the door closes. The defaults are W = 20 and D = 10, so a connection needs 30 minutes between gate arrival and pushback. The lookup tool lets you change W.

The shortcut

The outbound flight is often late too

The obvious way to judge a connection is to ask how often the inbound flight lands more than the spare time late. That ignores the outbound. When the inbound was 30 or more minutes late, the outbound left 15 or more minutes late {{depLateGiven}}% of the time, against {{depLateBase}}% of all pairs: the same storm and the same congested airport hold up both. Part of the gap does not depend on that link: outbound flights leave late on average, so even unrelated delays would shrink the shortcut's estimate. The correlation between the two delays is {{corrAll}} overall, from {{corrMin}} at {{corrMinHub}} to {{corrMax}} at {{corrMaxHub}}.

At 45 minutes the shortcut gives {{sc45naive}}% against a paired {{sc45paired}}%. The delay part alone overstates by {{sc45delayGap}} points ({{sc45naiveDelay}}% against {{sc45pairedDelay}}%), offset a little by the {{sc45depCancel}} points of outbound cancellations the shortcut never sees. The gap closes at about {{scCross}} minutes and reverses after it, when cancellations make up most of the remaining risk. By hub it runs from {{scHubGapMin}} points at {{scHubGapMinHub}} to {{scHubGapMax}} at {{scHubGapMaxHub}}.

Evaluation

Three estimators, tested on six months they never saw

Each estimator was fitted on September 2023 to February 2026 and scored on {{evalN}} pairs from March to August 2026, where {{evalObserved}}% were missed. The inbound-only shortcut uses the same cells and backoff as the cell average, so the two differ only in what they average. The logistic model has terms for hub and airline, layover, arrival hour, month and weekday, with layover, month and hour allowed to vary by hub; it was fitted on {{evalTrainN}} training pairs.

Limits

What this cannot tell you

  • Constructed pairs are not real bookings. Airlines do not sell every pair, and each sets a minimum connection time per airport. No public table of those minimums was found, so the tool greys layovers under 35 minutes rather than using each airline's figure.
  • One walking time per connection is a simplification. A gate change across a big airport can take much longer than 20 minutes, which is why W is a slider.
  • Only US domestic flights by the carriers that report to BTS. No international legs, no customs, no partner-airline connections.
  • Airlines sometimes hold a departure for connecting passengers. That appears here as outbound delay and cannot be told apart from ordinary delay.
  • A miss here means rebooking. How many hours it cost is not measured.
  • Pairs share arrivals and days, so the intervals shown are too narrow.
